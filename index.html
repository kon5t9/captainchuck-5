ENTRY 1 — THE CREDENTIALS30 years afloat · 23 in the engine room

Whoever is reading this at the dock

I learned navigation the expensive way: paper charts, a parallel rule and a bearing taken the moment the GPS looked bored. I learned engineering the same way, under a generator at 0300 with a bilge light in my teeth. Twenty-three years as a mechanic taught me which boats lie about themselves. Thirty years at the helm taught me which owners sleep well.

What I do is simple to describe and hard to fake: I bring the boat, the crew and the guests where they were supposed to be, on time, with the logbook filled in and nothing broken that I didn't have to break. Charter, delivery, standing watch at someone's season, refit oversight, teaching an owner to dock his own 70-footer in a crosswind. All of it runs on the same license and the same set of calluses.

Not a hobbyist. Experience counts — ask any captain for a resume and references. I'm happy to hand mine over.

ENTRY 5 — WHO SIGNS THE CHECKowners, buyers, lenders, principals

The people who end up calling

Boat owners, corporate or private, who need a charter captain for hire for a weekend or a season. Buyers who want their money on the far side of a sea trial. Brokers, insurers and lenders who need a captain whose license survives due diligence on the first try. Recovery agents who need a vessel moved and secured without a scene.

If you are interviewing captains, ask all of them for the credential number. The ones who stall are telling you something.

  • Owners. Standing assignments, relief weeks, deliveries, and management while the boat earns its upkeep.
  • Buyers & brokers. Pre-purchase inspections and survey sea trials from the buyer's side of the transom.
  • Insurers & lenders. Licensed, verifiable, consortium-tested: paperwork that clears underwriting.
  • Recovery agents. Repossession moves done by a captain, not a stunt.

ENTRY 7 — QUESTIONS RECEIVED BY RADIOand the answers, as logged

Radio traffic

Q — Can I verify your license?

Yes, and you should. USCG Master of Steam or Motor Vessels, 500 GT / 200 GRT, International, credential #2776127, verified free at the official Merchant Mariner Credential portal. Do it with everyone you interview, including me, twice if I seem evasive.

Q — How far will you take the boat?

Coastwise and ocean. Newport, San Diego, Marina del Rey and Long Beach are home ports; the log runs from Alaska down to the Gulf of Mexico via the Panama Canal, and up the East Coast from Florida to the Carolinas. Thousands of miles, both blue and coastal.

Q — What happens if something breaks at sea?

Twenty-three years as a mechanic — diesel, AC/DC electrical, hydraulics, crane operation — means most failures get fixed on the passage instead of ending it. The spares locker is planned before the spools are tripped, and the passage plan always has a harbor in it.

Q — Can you train me or my crew on our own boat?

That's exactly how the program works: your vessel, your waters. Handling, docking, navigation, radar, and the electronics you already own but don't yet trust. Insurance sign-off when that's the goal.

Q — Do you handle corporate charters and big groups?

Day charters to week-long cruises, private and corporate. Corporate work is just logistics with better wine: arrivals timed, fuel topped, crew briefed. The boat is the venue; I keep the venue running.

Q — What are your rates?

Depends on the boat, the miles, and the dates — day rates in California are not the ones you'd quote in the Mediterranean. Call and we'll do the arithmetic together in five minutes.
